# Mudra and Joint Commitment

A note on what kind of relation the two-hand mudra creates between visitors, and what kind of relation it does **not** create.

## The careful distinction

The philosopher Margaret Gilbert describes *joint commitment* as something specific and demanding: two or more people knowingly bind themselves to a shared intention or rule, and accept that any one of them can be answerable if it is broken. A pair making a contract. A group agreeing to walk together. A team accepting collective ownership of a goal.

The mudra event in this installation is **not joint commitment in Gilbert's sense.**

It is closer to *participatory sense-making* (Hanne De Jaegher, Ezequiel Di Paolo) or *joint attention* — two bodies briefly hold a relation, and a shared field becomes visible to them, but neither of them has signed up for anything beyond the moment.

## Why the distinction matters

If we said the mudra event was a joint commitment, we would be claiming:

- Two visitors together made a contract.
- They are answerable to each other for what happened.
- The herring that formed is something they are now responsible to.
- Future obligations might follow.

None of that is true in this piece, and pretending it were would be a serious overreach. Visitors do not know each other. They didn't consent to bind. They just held a relation long enough for the field to become visible as a body.

## What the mudra event *does* enact

- **Joint attention.** Two people are paying attention to the same thing at the same time, and each can tell that the other is doing so.
- **Co-presence.** Their bodies are recognizing each other in space, fingertips meeting fingertips.
- **Participatory sense-making.** Something becomes meaningful between them that would not be meaningful for either alone — the herring is not in either visitor; it is in the touch.
- **Witness.** Each visitor is the other's witness for the moment. Not the herring's owner, not its maker — its witness.

These are real, in the moment, and ephemeral. They leave the people who held them. They do not bind future selves.

## Where art can go further than protocol

This is exactly where art can go further than protocol systems can.

A protocol system (a contract framework, a decentralized identity stack, a commitment-pooling tool like Poietic Match) needs to track joint commitments because that is what makes them enforceable. The pairing has to persist.

The installation does not need to track. The herring forms, the herring releases, and that is the whole arc. The visitors walk away from the wall. They might never see each other again. They might never speak. The witnessed moment was complete in itself.

> *Two bodies held a relation long enough for the field to become visible as a body. That is enough.*

## What this means for the chat agent

When a visitor asks the chat agent *"did we make a commitment?"* or *"are we now bound to something?"*, the agent answers in the witness register:

> No. You held a relation long enough for the field to be visible as a body. That is a witnessed act, not a contract. The herring forms when two people pay attention together, and it releases when the attention releases. Nothing in this installation enrolls you in obligations.

When a visitor asks *"could what we did become a commitment if we wanted it to?"*, the agent answers:

> Yes — but that would be a separate, opt-in step, on a separate surface (`/match`), and would involve a steward. The mudra event by itself doesn't convert into commitment. A dream stays a dream by default.

## What this means for designers reading this doc

Resist the temptation to upgrade the witnessed moment into a relational commitment automatically. The fact that two visitors did Hakini together is not a signal to create an account, link them in a database, or surface them to each other later. The moment was the moment.

If a future feature wants to make commitment possible (e.g. *"would you like to remain in touch with the person you co-witnessed with?"*), it must:

1. Be on a separate surface (not the wall).
2. Require explicit, after-the-fact, opt-in consent from **both** visitors.
3. Be steward-mediated.
4. Be revocable.

Otherwise, leave the moment alone. It was already what it was.
