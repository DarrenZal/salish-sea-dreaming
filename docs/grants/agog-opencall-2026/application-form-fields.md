### Project overview: goal + impact *(~320 / 350)*
Salish Sea Dreaming is an immersive, real-time generative installation in which a living bioregion, the Salish Sea, uses technology to perceive itself. Visitors enter a darkened, ceremonial space where marine life (herring, salmon, orca) moves across a large projection in a living watercolor language, grounded in real ecological data: tide, lunar phase, and river discharge. Seventy-five years of herring records drive an original musical score. The work is built on co-dreaming, holding aspiration and ecological grief together. As the project's public site puts it: we are the Salish Sea, dreaming itself awake.

The work rewards witnessing, not performance: the visitor becomes a witness to the sea, a counter to the attention economy. Where venues allow, visitors co-dream: through a phone app and gesture interaction they offer a few words for the sea, and those words join a live, semantically clustered, consent-governed archive of everyone's dreams. This collective-memory layer ran continuously for more than eight hours at our May 2026 installation.

The ambition is not to tour one finished piece. Each installation is co-created with the place it lands in. We bring the technical scaffolding and then build the actual work with local artists and that watershed's own ecological data. That is how the first two were made: the April Salt Spring installation grew from a local naturalist-illustration lineage and Salish Sea herring records; the May Vancouver installation was co-authored with a Coast Salish artist under a per-output consent protocol. The protocol travels; the dreaming stays rooted.

We hope for:
- Reach: 8 to 10 community installations, an estimated 2,000+ visitors (about 200 per stop).
- Paid co-creation at every stop with a local artist and local data, so the work is of that place, not dropped in.
- A real, local pathway into restoration offered at each stop through the host landscape group, with uptake reported rather than promised.
- The consent-governed dream archive as a living, qualitative record of what the work surfaces in each place.
- Replication: at least one other bioregion takes up the open technical toolkit and makes it its own.

### Generative AI: which tools + how *(~114 / 150)*
Yes. The installation's visuals are generated live in real time. We use StyleGAN2-ada, via Autolume (the open-source tool from SFU's MetaCreation Lab), to train species-specific models on Creative Commons licensed local biodiversity imagery, navigated in real time. We use Stable Diffusion with StreamDiffusion in TouchDesigner for real-time painterly rendering, with a naturalist-watercolor aesthetic developed through LoRA fine-tuning. Training data is restricted to CC0, CC-BY, and CC-BY-SA sources and explicitly consented imagery.

Critically, AI is a tool, not a replacement. Coast Salish visual grammar is authored by the artist under a per-output consent protocol; it is never AI-generated. Indigenous knowledge and cultural design are held outside the model and governed by the people who hold them. The living record at each show is the co-dreaming archive, the words visitors offer the sea, not a citizen-science dataset; linking it to deeper bioregional data is a direction we are exploring.

### Immersive media format(s) and how they are used *(~165 / 200)*
Salish Sea Dreaming is a room-scale immersive installation, experienced together in a shared physical space rather than through a headset.
- Real-time generative projection: large-scale projected visuals (typically a 3 to 6 metre wall), generated live rather than played back, so the imagery never repeats.
- Spatial and ambient audio: an evolving musical score driven by ecological data, enveloping the room as a soundscape.
- Browser-based gesture interaction: where venues allow, visitors shape a shared dream-field through hand gestures sensed entirely on their own phone (no headset, no room camera), and offer a few words that join a live, consent-governed archive.
- A live web experience at salishseadreaming.art that extends the work beyond the room.

The grant-funded capstone adds a dome and large-format (fulldome) projection pipeline. We deliberately avoid headset AR and VR: the work is about collective, embodied presence in a place a whole community enters together, not a device you put on alone.

### Experience from the participant's perspective *(~190)*
A visitor steps out of the daylight into a darkened, quiet, ceremonial space, closer to a meditation room than a screen. A large projection surrounds them: marine life in a living watercolor language, herring, salmon, an orca, moving as one shared field of relations, generated live and never repeating. The room fills with sound, an evolving score composed from seventy-five years of the sea's own data, so they hear the history of the place as music.

The work rewards witnessing rather than performance. There is nothing to win and no screen to swipe; the longer a visitor stays, the more they settle into witnessing the sea.

Where the venue allows, a key moment arrives: the visitor is invited to co-dream. On their own phone, a simple hand gesture and a few words offered to the water. Their dream joins a living, semantically clustered, consent-governed archive, a visible field of everyone's hopes and griefs for the sea.

The experience closes by turning feeling toward action: a door into restoration work already happening in the visitor's own watershed.

## CLIMATE RELEVANCE

### The story + climate connection + why now *(~259 / 300)*
Our first story is the Salish Sea's, the inland ocean shared by British Columbia and Washington, through one of its keystones: the Pacific herring. Forage fish like herring are the base of a climate-regulating food web, the trophic cascade that feeds salmon, that feeds orca, that feeds the forest through carcass nutrients, that sequesters carbon. Forage fish are worth more alive in the water than landed. Markets cannot price that value; a quota system sees only the fish in the net.

The installation also makes the loss audible. Composer Matt Robertson translated seventy-five years of herring catch and spawn-collapse records into an evolving ten-minute musical score: rising catch lifts the pitch, a steady pulse marks each passing year, and the collapsing spawn data pulls the tone toward an other-worldly, underwater darkness. It is the history of the herring rendered as sound. The silence at the end is not the end. It is where the dreaming begins.

Why now: this is a story about the gap between knowing and feeling. We are saturated with climate data and starved of climate feeling, and feeling is what precedes action. The Salish Sea is simultaneously breathtakingly alive and catastrophically wounded. An orca carried her dead calf for seventeen days while the world watched. The work refuses to resolve that tension into despair or false hope. It holds both, and offers, to those who want it, a door into the restoration work already happening in their own watershed. In a moment of climate grief and paralysis, audiences need an experience that lets them feel the loss and, if they choose, find a next step, inside the very bioregion where they are standing. Herring is the Salish Sea's keystone; each watershed surfaces its own.

### Climate expertise / research / lived experience *(~144 / 200)*
The work is grounded in real ecological data and knowledge systems.
- **Western science:** Fisheries and Oceans Canada (DFO) catch records since 1888 and seventy-five years of spawn-index data, salmon escapement counts, hydrophone networks, and tidal and river monitoring. These are the datasets that drive the visuals and the musical score.
- **Shifting-baseline research** sits at the heart of the piece: DFO's 1953 management baseline was already a depleted year, so seventy years of quotas were calibrated to an impoverished sea.
- **Indigenous ecological knowledge and data sovereignty:** the leadership of coastal nations who challenged federal stock assessments and built their own, and the Indigenomics principle (Carol Anne Hilton) that life, not GDP, is the primary unit of value.
- **Community mapping lineage:** the work descends from participatory bioregional mapping in this same sea, including the 2005 Islands in the Salish Sea Community Atlas, where thousands of residents across seventeen islands mapped what they cherish and local artists rendered each island by hand. Each of our installations carries that practice into a new watershed.
- **Lived experience:** the creative team lives and works in the Salish Sea bioregion, with a naturalist-illustration lineage rooted in decades of observing these waters.

### Voices / communities centered + authenticity, consent, relationship *(~165 / 200)*
The project centers the Salish Sea bioregion itself and the Indigenous and place-based communities in relationship with it. Our approach to consent is structural.
- **Coast Salish cultural co-authorship under a per-output consent protocol.** The cultural visual grammar is authored by a Coast Salish artist who approves every use, per source, per workflow, per output, including public-facing language. AI never substitutes for that authorship. We name the artist publicly only once they have approved sponsor-facing language.
- **Indigenous data sovereignty (OCAP).** Indigenous knowledge stays rooted with the communities who hold it. It is explicitly not open-sourced. Our open toolkit shares only the technical scaffolding.
- **Relationship before scale, at the speed of trust.** Our engagement begins with individual Indigenous knowledge holders and artists, not with claims of formal Nation partnership. These relationships require patience, presence, and protocol; they are led by Carol Anne Hilton (Indigenomics Institute), and they cannot be compressed to a funding timeline. Honoraria for Indigenous knowledge holders and cultural advisors are budgeted, and each relationship proceeds at its own pace and discretion.

## AUDIENCE AND REACH

### Whom is this project for? *(~158 / 200)*
Three concentric audiences.
1. **Place-based Salish Sea communities:** the watershed-scale landscape groups in the Regenerate Cascadia Landscape Hub Cultivator network. Our US anchor hosts, connected through fiscal sponsor Department of Bioregion, are the Salish Sea groups in Whatcom, Skagit, Whidbey Island, Seattle, Vashon, and the Olympic Peninsula, extending across the border to Vancouver and Victoria. These are people already doing restoration and stewardship, for whom the work can connect feeling to the labor they already give their watershed.
2. **The wider gathering of climate and bioregional organizers** convening at the Turtle Island Bioregional Congress (Vernonia, Oregon, September 2026, about 300 attendees), where the work will appear as a demonstration.
3. **The general public** at each host venue: people who would never attend a climate lecture but will sit inside a beautiful, grieving, hopeful sea and walk out moved, and with a way to act.

We most want to reach people in the gap between knowing, feeling, and acting, and the on-the-ground restoration workers who rarely get to experience the meaning of what they protect.
